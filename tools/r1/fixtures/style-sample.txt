There's a particular kind of evening light in late September — you'll know it if you've ever stood at a kitchen window around six and watched it land on the floor — that does something to the air. The room doesn't get warmer, exactly. It gets denser. As though somebody had taken all the small private feelings that hang around a house — the half-finished conversations, the books left open on the arm of the couch, the laundry you keep meaning to fold — and pressed them gently into the floorboards until they became part of the wood.

I've been trying for years to describe it without sounding like I'm selling something. The honest answer is that I can't. The light is the light. You either notice it or you don't, and if you don't, no description will make you. If you do, you already know what I mean, and the description is unnecessary.

What I can say — what I think I can say — is that on those evenings I am, for about twenty minutes, the version of myself I would most like to be. Patient. Unhurried. Reasonable about the things I usually find unreasonable. Then the light shifts and I am somebody else again, somebody who needs to start dinner, somebody who has not answered three emails. But for those twenty minutes I am the right kind of person, and I think the light is a sort of gift, and I am grateful for it, even though I do not know whom to thank.
