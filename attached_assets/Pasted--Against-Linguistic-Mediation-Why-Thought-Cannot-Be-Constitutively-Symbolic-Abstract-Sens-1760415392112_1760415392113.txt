# Against Linguistic Mediation: Why Thought Cannot Be Constitutively Symbolic

## Abstract

Sense-perceptions do not have to be deciphered if their contents are to be uploaded, the reason being that they are presentations, not representations. Linguistic expressions do have to be deciphered if their contents are to be uploaded, the reason being that they are representations, not presentations. It is viciously regressive to suppose that information-bearing mental entities are categorically in the nature of representations, as opposed to presentations, and it is therefore incoherent to suppose that thought is mediated by expressions or, therefore, by linguistic entities. Attempts to neutralize this criticism inevitably overextend the concept of what it is to be a linguistic symbol, the result being that such attempts eviscerate the very position that it is their purpose to defend. Also, it is inherent in the nature of such attempts that they assume the truth of the view that for a given mental entity to bear this as opposed to that information is for that entity to have this as opposed to that causal role. This view is demonstrably false, dooming to failure the just-mentioned attempts to defend the contention that thought is in all cases mediated by linguistic symbols.

## 1. The Presentation-Representation Distinction

The distinction between presentations and representations is not merely classificatory but marks a fundamental difference in how mental contents become cognitively available. A presentation delivers its content directly to cognition without requiring an intermediate decoding operation. When you experience pain, the quale itself *is* the cognitive uptake—there is no gap between the vehicle and its payload that must be bridged by interpretation. By contrast, a representation stands proxy for its content and requires a decoding operation to make that content cognitively accessible. The sentence "snow is white" does not directly present whiteness or snow; it encodes information that must be linguistically processed before its propositional content becomes available to thought.

This distinction maps onto Bertrand Russell's knowledge by acquaintance versus knowledge by description, though the present formulation is more demanding. For Russell, one could have acquaintance with sense-data while descriptions involved propositional knowledge. The presentation-representation distinction operates at a more fundamental level: it concerns the *mechanism* by which content becomes cognitively accessible, not merely the epistemic directness of our relation to objects. A presentation requires no interpretive apparatus to deliver its content; a representation necessarily does.

The neurological evidence supports this bifurcation. V1 neurons responding to oriented edges do not "decode" or "interpret" retinal stimulation—they transduce it. The firing pattern itself constitutes the presentational content available to downstream processing. There is no homunculus consulting a codebook to determine what the firing pattern "means." Conversely, processing the sentence "the edge is vertical" involves Wernicke's area, inferior frontal regions, and semantic memory systems precisely because extracting propositional content from linguistic form requires computational work that bridging retinal stimulation to V1 does not. The asymmetry is not merely one of complexity but of kind.

## 2. The Regress Argument

Suppose, per the linguistic mediation thesis, that all cognitive content is carried by linguistic or quasi-linguistic representations. Then to think that snow is white, one must tokening an internal sentence like "snow is white." But this internal sentence is itself a representation—a string of symbols that encodes rather than presents its content. For this representation to deliver its content to cognition, it must itself be interpreted or decoded. But interpretation is itself a cognitive operation that bears content (roughly: "this symbol means that"). If all cognitive content is representational, then this interpretive operation must itself be carried by a linguistic representation, which must itself be interpreted, which requires another representation, ad infinitum.

The standard reply invokes "implicit" or "tacit" understanding: the internal sentence is understood directly without requiring a further interpretive act. But this reply concedes the game. If the internal sentence can be understood "directly" without interpretation, then we have admitted that some mental contents—namely, whatever constitutes this direct understanding—are presentational rather than representational. They deliver their content without requiring decoding. And if presentational mental contents are possible for understanding linguistic representations, there is no principled reason they could not constitute thought directly, bypassing linguistic mediation entirely.

One might object that "understanding" a representation is not itself a contentful mental state but merely a functional disposition—the disposition to draw appropriate inferences, make appropriate utterances, and so forth. But this dispositional account faces two problems. First, dispositions are individuated by their manifestations, yet the manifestations in question (drawing inferences, making utterances) are themselves contentful mental acts. We have simply relocated the problem. Second, and more fundamentally, if understanding merely consists in having the right dispositions, then any system with the right input-output profile counts as understanding, which collapses into behaviorism. The Chinese Room argument demonstrates that syntactic manipulation plus appropriate input-output mappings does not constitute semantic understanding. Whatever differentiates genuine understanding from mere symbol-shuffling cannot itself be cashed out in purely dispositional or causal-functional terms.

## 3. The Overextension Problem

Defenders of linguistic mediation sometimes respond by liberalizing what counts as "linguistic." Perhaps thought is mediated by symbols, but these symbols need not be language-like in the sense of possessing syntactic structure, compositionality, or conventional semantics. Perhaps any systematically contentful mental particular counts as a "symbol" in the relevant sense.

This move is self-defeating. The linguistic mediation thesis gains whatever plausibility it has from the observation that public language clearly does mediate some thought—I can think about Platonic forms or quantum superposition only because I have acquired the linguistic resources to represent these contents. The thesis extends this model to cognition generally. But if we dilute "linguistic" to mean merely "systematically contentful," we have abandoned what made linguistic mediation explanatory. Mental states that are systematically contentful but non-representational—that is, presentations with systematic inferential or recognitional roles—now count as "linguistic symbols." But presentations, as established, do not require decoding. So the revised thesis asserts only that thought is mediated by mental contents, which is trivial.

Consider the parallel with computationalism. Early computational theories of mind posited that cognition involves rule-governed manipulation of syntactically structured representations—genuinely language-like mental sentences. Critics pointed out that this requires a symbolic level of description, which may not capture subsymbolic processes like pattern recognition or perceptual discrimination. Defenders responded by broadening "computation" to include any information-processing, including connectionist networks without explicit symbolic structure. But at this level of generality, "computation" becomes coextensive with "causation in systems describable information-theoretically," which robs computationalism of explanatory content. The same fate befalls linguistic mediation when "linguistic" is stretched to cover any contentful mental particular.

## 4. The Causal Role Objection

The overextension strategy typically relies on a causal-functional account of content: what makes a mental state M bear content C is that M occupies a certain causal role—it is typically produced by certain stimuli and typically produces certain behavioral or cognitive effects. On this view, the distinction between presentations and representations collapses, since both are individuated by their causal profiles. A visual experience of red has the content it does because it is caused by red surfaces and disposed to produce certain judgments and behaviors. A mental token of the word "red" has its content for structurally similar reasons. So there is no deep metaphysical divide between presentational and representational content.

This argument fails. Causal role is at best necessary for content determination but cannot be sufficient. Consider Twin Earth scenarios. On Earth, "water" is caused by and causes behavior toward H₂O. On Twin Earth, "water" (or "retaw") is caused by and causes behavior toward XYZ. The causal roles are functionally isomorphic, yet the contents differ—one is about H₂O, the other about XYZ. Content must therefore outrun causal role. The standard response invokes wide content and environmental factors, but this only reinforces the point: causal role internal to the cognitive system underdetermines content.

More decisively, causal role theories face the problem of error and misrepresentation. If M has content C in virtue of being typically caused by C-type stimuli, what happens when M is tokened in the absence of such stimuli? The causal theorist says M then misrepresents. But this presupposes that M already has determinate content C such that C's absence constitutes misrepresentation. We cannot derive M's having content C from causal regularities while simultaneously appealing to M's having content C to explain why deviations from those regularities count as error. The account is circular.

Presentations avoid this problem. A pain experience has its qualitative character intrinsically, not in virtue of its causal role. Evolutionary biology explains why tissue damage typically causes pain (organisms that feel pain when injured avoid further injury), but the phenomenal what-it's-like-ness of pain does not consist in this causal-evolutionary story. Even a brain in a vat experiencing pain has a mental state with determinate phenomenal content. Representations, by contrast, have their content only derivatively—a mark on paper means nothing until interpreted by a mind that grasps what it represents. So causal role cannot erase the presentation-representation distinction; it merely highlights that representations require an explanatory base that presentations provide.

## 5. Empirical Evidence Against Linguistic Mediation

The linguistic mediation thesis predicts that conceptual thought should be impossible without language. Yet multiple lines of evidence contradict this.

First, prelinguistic infants demonstrate sophisticated conceptual capacities. Twelve-month-olds who have not yet acquired language distinguish between agents and non-agents based on goal-directed behavior (Woodward 1998), track numerical quantities approximately (Wynn 1992), and reason about physical occlusion and object permanence (Baillargeon 1987). These capacities require contentful mental representations of goals, numbers, and persistent objects. The representations cannot be linguistic, since the infants lack language.

One might reply that these are not genuine thoughts but mere sensorimotor schemas or associative expectations. But this reply is unmotivated. When an infant reaches for an object hidden behind a screen, she demonstrates an expectation that the object persists during occlusion. This expectation has correctness conditions (it can be correct or incorrect depending on whether the object is actually there) and plays a role in guiding action. It is contentful in precisely the sense that thoughts are. Denying that prelinguistic infants think requires either accepting a linguistic chauvinism whereby thought is stipulated to require language, or providing an alternative account of what differentiates genuine thought from these infant capacities. No such account has been forthcoming.

Second, adult cognition in non-linguistic domains provides further evidence. Mental rotation tasks (Shepard & Metzler 1971) involve imagining an object rotating in three-dimensional space. Subjects report experiencing the rotation continuously, and response times increase linearly with rotation angle, suggesting analog spatial representation. This spatial thinking does not plausibly involve subvocalizing linguistic descriptions like "the object rotates 45 degrees clockwise." The phenomenology is imagistic, and the computational profile is analog rather than discrete-symbolic.

Similarly, expert intuition in domains like chess or musical improvisation operates too rapidly for linguistic mediation. Grandmasters apprehend board positions in 2-3 seconds (Chase & Simon 1973), far too quickly for linguistic encoding of piece positions and strategic implications. The recognition is direct and presentational—the master *sees* the position as threatening or advantageous without verbalizing why.

Third, patients with global aphasia who have lost productive and receptive language capacities nonetheless demonstrate preserved conceptual abilities. They can solve non-verbal reasoning problems, recognize depicted objects and actions, and pantomime tool use (Varley & Siegal 2000). If thought were constitutively linguistic, severe aphasia should eliminate thought. That it does not suggests thought can proceed without linguistic mediation.

The linguistic mediation theorist might respond that these subjects retain a "language of thought" even if they have lost public language. But if the language of thought is sufficiently unlike public language—lacking phonology, syntax, conventional semantics—then calling it "language" is the overextension move already critiqued. If instead Mentalese is genuinely language-like, then we face the regress problem: what interprets the Mentalese symbols?

## 6. Conclusion

Thought cannot be constitutively linguistic because linguistic representations require interpretation to deliver their contents, and interpretation is itself a cognitive act. Positing interpretation all the way down leads to vicious regress. The only escape is to admit presentational mental contents that deliver their information directly without requiring decoding. Once we admit presentations, there is no principled reason to deny that they can constitute thought directly, bypassing linguistic mediation. Attempts to salvage linguistic mediation by broadening "linguistic" to cover any contentful mental state either render the thesis trivial or collapse into causal-functional theories of content that demonstrably fail. Empirical evidence from prelinguistic infants, non-linguistic adult cognition, and aphasia further confirms that thought operates independently of language. The linguistic mediation thesis should therefore be rejected.
